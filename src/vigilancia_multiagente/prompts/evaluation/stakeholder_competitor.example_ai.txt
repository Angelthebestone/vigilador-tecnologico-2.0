The thesis hands an unearned competitive moat to three incumbent HSM vendors purely on the basis of installed footprint, while ignoring that PQC migration is precisely the kind of architecture refresh that resets switching costs and invites adjacent entrants. A 60–80% performance overhead is not a defensible advantage; it is a vulnerability to any competitor that lands closer to RSA-baseline performance, and the regional banks-as-a-service layer is shallow enough that re-platforming during a forced migration is materially cheaper than the report implies. The framing also undercounts the hyperscalers: AWS KMS, GCP Cloud KMS, and Azure Key Vault are already shipping PQC primitives at marginal price, and their bundling with the rest of the regional banks' cloud spend constitutes a cross-subsidization play that no specialized HSM vendor can match on TCO.

If I were running a competing HSM business, I would underprice the migration window aggressively, lock in 3-year FIPS-203 service contracts with revenue-share rather than capex pricing, and target the 5 of 14 banks that did NOT declare a roadmap — those are the late movers most exposed to a turnkey offer. The report's "9 of 14 declared" figure is, from a competitive lens, a list of accounts I would attack next.

- Hyperscaler omission: AWS/GCP/Azure PQC offerings are a substitute, not a complement, and create a TCO floor the named HSM vendors cannot defend; the report should quantify their cross-subsidization risk.
- Switching costs reset during migration: PQC re-platforming is a once-per-decade event during which lock-in evaporates; existing footprint is a temporary product edge, not a moat.
- Time-to-market window is shorter than implied: with FIPS 203/204/205 already final, a competent rival can ship a regional offering in 6–9 months, well inside the 2026 acceleration window.
- The 5 banks without a declared roadmap are an undefended segment: they are the highest-value acquisition target for any insurgent, and the report's positioning ignores them.
- Pricing power is fragile: a 60–80% performance overhead translates into compute cost pass-through that customers will benchmark across vendors, eroding any premium the incumbents try to charge.
